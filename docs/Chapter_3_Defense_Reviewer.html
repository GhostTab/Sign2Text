The whole system in two minutes

Say this if they ask “what did you build?”
Sign2Text is an Android application. It reads one hand from the phone camera and shows the matching letter of the American Sign Language fingerspelling alphabet, A to Z. We trained the model on a computer. After installation, recognition runs on the phone. No image, landmark, or letter is sent to a server.

The path of one frame is the sentence the whole chapter is built on. Memorize it in this order.

Camera frame → 21 hand landmarks → 95 numbers → small neural network → accept the letter or leave the screen blank → show the letter after 3 agreeing frames → write it into the word after 6 frames, and only if spelling is turned on

We built it in rounds. A small app came first. Real signing then showed what had to change: the training photos were too dark, the camera would not switch, some letters were hard, a closed O was read as C, a resting hand became a letter, and people draw Z in the air while many training photos only show a pointing finger. Each of those became the next change. That is why the method is iterative and incremental, following Sommerville (2016), and not a waterfall plan.

Keep this limit in the answer. The app spells letters. It does not read a whole-word sign such as the waved greeting for “Hi.” The dataset contains about 300 short videos, including Hello, Thank You, Sorry, Yes, and No. We did not train on those videos. A model trained on still alphabet photos cannot recognize a moving word sign.

3.1 Project / Research Design

In one breath. This is applied research and development. The question is whether a phone can turn a held ASL letter into text without a network, and whether the screen can stay blank when the hand is only resting. The result is a working Android application and the training procedure that produced its classifier.

What the two phases are

Table 3.1 splits the work. Offline development happens on a computer in Python and produces the landmark table, the trained network, the normalization file, the calibration file, and the TensorFlow Lite model. Use on the phone produces a letter on the screen and, if spelling is on, a word built from letters the signer holds. The phone does not retrain the network. It loads one fixed bundle that was copied in at build time.

Why landmarks, not the photo itself

MediaPipe Hands gives 21 points on the hand. Each point has x, y, and z, so that is 63 numbers (Zhang et al., 2020). We move the wrist to the origin, scale by the longest wrist-to-point distance, and mirror a left hand so it sits in the same frame as a right hand. We then add 20 distances from the wrist and 12 offsets from the thumb tip to the middle joints of the four fingers. Fist letters look alike in raw coordinates. The thumb position is what separates them. Before the network, each of the 95 values is turned into a z-score with the training mean and standard deviation. A z-score means “how far is this value from the training average, in units of the training spread,” so the phone and the training script use the same scale.

What are the variables?The inputs we observe are the hand pose, which camera, the light, and the distance. We do not control hand size. 3.2 Software Development Methodology

In one breath. We followed iterative and incremental development, as described by Sommerville in the 10th edition of Software Engineering (2016). We released a small system, used it, and changed it. Requirements were not frozen before the first build.

Figure 3.1, the cycle

Six boxes, left to right: Requirements, Design, Build, Test, Release, Feedback. The orange arrow under the boxes returns from Feedback to Requirements. The caption on the figure says what failed in use becomes the next requirement. Feedback from one cycle is the input to the next.

It is incremental because each cycle left a usable application in place. The spelling screen, the camera, and the model were not postponed until a final stage. It is iterative because the thresholds were revised after they failed with a real hand.

Table 3.4, the eight cycles, in the order to retell them

  1. First recognizer. Landmarks and a small network could show a letter. We built the camera, the welcome and recognition screens, and Save and Clear. The input grew from 63 values to 95.
  2. Training images. Dark, side-lit crops did not match a palm held toward a phone. We retrained on SignAlphaSet and replaced the model in the app.
  3. Camera. The preview surface swallowed taps, so a button drawn on the picture did not flip the camera. A full-screen tap target was added. A double tap switches front and back. The front-camera frame is mirrored before analysis.
  4. Hard letters. C, M, N, O, T, and Z were rejected when the sign was slightly off. A later build then printed letters when nobody had signed. Those letters may appear at a slightly lower score, but only when they are clearly first. A second-place letter can no longer replace the first.
  5. O read as C. A closed circle and an open curve share a silhouette. If the network already prefers C or O, the thumb-to-index gap chooses between them.
  6. Resting hand. An idle hand was displayed and then written into the word. We now wait 3 agreeing frames before a letter is shown, and 6 before it is recorded. The word is saved only with the Save control.
  7. Drawn Z. Z in the photographs is often a still point, while signers draw the letter. A short path check on the index fingertip was added. A still Z from the network is also allowed.
  8. Release. Classmates who need the source pull the repository. People who only need the app install the debug package from a GitHub release.
U and P are in the current rule, and they are not their own rows in Table 3.4. Cycle 4 records the first hard-letter pass: C, M, N, O, T, and Z. After live signing, U was added because it was often confused with R, and P was added because a held P was hard to keep on screen. If the panel asks, point to Table 3.16 and Figure 3.5. U appears only when U is already the top guess. If the top guess is R, the screen stays on R. P appears only when P is already the top guess. If the top guess is K, the screen stays on K.

3.3 Requirements Analysis

In one breath. Two roles use the app, and they may be the same person. The signer fingerspells, may turn spelling on, and chooses the camera. The reader reads the letter or the saved word and does not need an account or a second device.

Functional requirements are what the app does. They are FR-01 through FR-14 in Table 3.5. Non-functional requirements are the qualities: privacy, offline use, speed, which phones, a single install, repeatable training, a blank screen when unsure, and portrait only. Those are NFR-01 through NFR-08 in Table 3.6. Constraints in Table 3.8 are limits we accepted, such as one hand, no cloud budget, and a still-image training set.

The fourteen functions, shortened so you can list them

  1. Welcome screen first. Recognition starts from there.
  2. Camera only after permission.
  3. Live preview and a letter together.
  4. One hand tracked.
  5. The letter is one of A to Z. Index 0 is A. Index 25 is Z.
  6. An unclear hand stays blank.
  7. No hand in frame clears the live letter.
  8. Double tap on the preview switches camera.
  9. Spelling can be turned on, and it is off by default.

3.4 System Architecture

In one breath. Sign2Text is a single-user mobile application with the processing inside the package. In a web system, “backend” means a server. In this study the processing layer is on the phone. The training computer produced the model bundle once. The phone does not call that computer again.

Figure 3.2, the three columns

  • Left, phone interface: welcome screen, camera preview, letter and status, spelling sheet, Save, Backspace, and Clear.
  • Middle, on-device pipeline: CameraX frame, MediaPipe’s 21 landmarks, 95 features then a z-score, the TensorFlow Lite network and the gates, then smooth, confirm, and record.
  • Right, dashed orange box: the training computer. It is labeled “not used at runtime.” SignAlphaSet images, landmark extraction, MLP training, TensorFlow Lite export, and the asset bundle inside the APK. The arrow goes from that bundle into the phone. It does not point the other way.

Figure 3.3 and Table 3.9, one frame

Read the boxes left to right: Frame, Hand landmarks, 95-D features, Network logits, Softmax and average, Accept or blank, Show then maybe record. If a stage cannot support a letter, that frame stays blank. It is not assigned a guess.

The pieces, in the order a frame meets them:

  1. CameraX supplies the preview and a frame for analysis. Front-camera frames are mirrored.
  2. Hand Landmarker runs in live-stream mode, one hand, confidence 0.20. It returns 21 landmarks or nothing.
  3. HandPreprocessor makes the 95 values. The thumb–index gap for O and C is read here, before the z-score, because the z-score would change the raw distance.
  4. FeatureNormalizer applies the training mean and standard deviation.
  5. TFLiteClassifier returns 26 logits. A logit is the raw score before it becomes a probability. Softmax turns those 26 scores into probabilities that add up to 1. The formula is softmax of logit divided by T, and the shipped T is 1.0.
  6. Smoother keeps an exponential moving average. The new frame has weight 0.70, so a new pose is not ignored and one bad frame does not take over.
  7. LetterAcceptance and ZStrokeDetector return a letter or no letter.
  8. LetterDisplayDebouncer shows the letter after 3 agreeing frames and returns to blank after 3 rejected frames.
  9. LetterSequenceAccumulator appends the letter after 6 identical displayed frames, once per hold.
  10. WordDictionary only comments on the string already recorded. It does not choose the letter.

Why landmarks instead of pixels

A network trained on raw pixels would also have to learn skin color, background, and camera distance. Landmarks already remove most of that. The phone and the training script share one definition of the 95 values, and a parity script checks that the two definitions agree. The cost of this choice is that M and N remain easy to confuse, and a motion that is not in the still photos, such as a J flick, is invisible to the network. For Z, a second detector covers part of the motion.

What “integration” means here

CameraX, MediaPipe, and TensorFlow Lite are compiled into the package. Those are the integrations. At runtime the app does not call a website, a login service, or a cloud model. The six files in Table 3.10 are already inside the install.

3.5 System Design and Modeling

In one breath. The signer starts recognition from the welcome screen. Seeing the live letter is the main case. Spelling, saving, and editing are optional. The English word list does not change the letter on screen.

Figure 3.4, the use cases

The stick figure is the Signer. The ovals inside the Sign2Text box are: Start recognition, Allow the camera, See the live letter, Switch camera, Turn spelling on, Hold a letter, Save the word, Backspace, and Clear. The orange oval, “Check the English word list,” is supporting behavior. Say that sentence if they ask why it is a different color.

Figure 3.6, the wireframes

Two portrait phones. The left one is Welcome: the name Sign2Text, a short explanation, the button “Let’s start,” and the line “On-device · private · offline.” The welcome screen does not open the camera. The right one is Recognition: the camera fills the screen, a translucent bar at the top, and a sheet at the bottom that peeks with the letter and the line “Swipe up for Save and Clear.” Save and Clear are not drawn on that first peek, because the user has to swipe to reveal them. A double tap on the camera area switches the camera. A double tap on the bars does not, because the header and the sheet are drawn above the tap layer so their buttons still work.

Figure 3.5, the decision order — this is the figure to know cold

Walk it from the top.

  1. Are hand landmarks present? If no, the screen is blank and the Z detector resets.
  2. Was a Z drawn in the last 1.8 seconds? If yes, the candidate is Z. This is decided first.
  3. Is the top letter C or O, with score at least 0.55 and a lead of at least 0.12? If the thumb-to-index gap is under 0.42, the letter is O. If the gap is over 0.48, the letter is C. When this rule returns O or C, the later score rules are not consulted.
  4. A gap between 0.42 and 0.48 does not reject the frame and does not force O or C. The next rule can still accept the model’s top letter.
  5. Is the top letter one of C, M, N, O, P, T, U, or Z, with score at least 0.55 and lead at least 0.12, and is that letter already first? If yes, accept it.
  6. For every other letter: score at least 0.60, lead at least 0.18, and entropy at most ln(26) × 0.75, about 2.44.
  7. If none of those match, there is no letter this frame.
  8. Any accepted letter, including Z and the O-versus-C result, is shown only after 3 agreeing frames.
  9. It is written into the word only when spelling mode is on and that same letter has stayed on screen for 6 frames.

The lead is the probability of the first letter minus the probability of the second. Entropy measures how spread the 26 probabilities are. When entropy is high, the network is unsure across many letters, so the frame is rejected even if the top score looks moderate. A second-place letter cannot steal the decision.

How to demonstrate O, C, U, and Z with your hand

  • O: a closed circle, fingertips together.
  • C: the same curve with a clear gap between thumb and index.
  • U: palm toward the camera, index and middle fingers together. U uses the easier rule only when U is already first. If the model’s top guess is R, the screen stays on R.
  • P: palm toward the camera and hold still. Point the index finger sideways, drop the middle finger, and keep the thumb out. P uses the easier rule only when P is already first. If the model’s top guess is K, the screen stays on K.
  • Z: point the index and draw a Z, or hold a still Z that the network already accepts. A straight wave is not a Z. The detector wants two horizontal strokes and a downward diagonal, at least 6 samples, a path longer than 1.05 times the hand scale, inside a 1.8-second window, then held for 0.8 seconds. The front camera is mirrored first, so a Z drawn on either camera is tested in the same orientation.

On the extracted SignAlphaSet rows, C and O separate cleanly on the gap. For C, the 10th percentile is about 0.52 and the median is about 0.67. For O, the 90th percentile is about 0.20 and the median is about 0.18. The cutoffs 0.42 and 0.48 sit in the empty interval between those two groups. The gap itself is not one of the 95 trained features. It is the distance between landmark 4, the thumb tip, and landmark 8, the index tip.

The network, in plain words

The classifier is a fully connected network, also called a multilayer perceptron. It does not output a letter directly. It outputs 26 logits, and softmax is applied outside the model so the temperature can be changed without retraining. Training used Adam, categorical cross-entropy, label smoothing of 0.05, balanced class weights, and a second copy of each training row with a random flip, a little noise, and a small scale change. The geometry features are recomputed after that change. Early stopping watches validation accuracy and restores the best weights.

Temperature scaling was fitted on the validation logits and hit 0.2, the bottom of the search, because near-duplicate frames make the network look more certain than a new signer will be. Shipping 0.2 would have pushed almost every frame through the confidence gate. The released calibration file uses 1.0, so the gates stay in force.

Save is an explicit button. An earlier version treated an open palm as a confirm gesture. Many real letters also look open, and that gesture was clearing a word before the signer had finished. That gesture was removed.

3.6 Database Design

In one breath. Sign2Text does not use a database server, and it does not use SQLite. Recognition has to work offline, the spelled text is needed only for the current visit, and camera frames must not be kept. A schema of users, sessions, and stored images would have created data the application is not meant to keep.

Figure 3.7, two boxes

The left box is shipped with the application and is read-only: the hand landmarker, the float model and the label map, the normalization and calibration files, and the word list loaded into a trie of 10,000 words. A trie is a tree of letters, so the app can tell that the letters so far are still the start of a word without scanning the whole list every frame. The right box is orange because it exists only while the app is open: smoothed probabilities, Z samples, the displayed letter, the spelling buffer, and the saved string. Closing the screen drops them. There is no history of words across visits.

Figure 3.8, the numbers on the lines

Those numbers are cardinality. They say how many of one thing belong to how many of another. They are not scores, versions, or accuracy.

3.7 Development Tools and Technologies

In one breath. The phone app is native Android, written in Kotlin. We did not use a web view, Flutter, or React Native, because the camera, MediaPipe, and TensorFlow Lite are available there without a second runtime. Training is Python. The two sides meet only at the asset files.

Versions the panel may ask you to find in Table 3.19: Java bytecode target 17, Android compile and target 34, minimum 26, Gradle 8.7, CameraX 1.3.4, MediaPipe Tasks Vision 0.10.14, TensorFlow Lite 2.16.1, JUnit 4.13.2, Python 3.12 in the project environment. The package name is com.sign2text.

The training order is fixed, and another person can rerun it:

  1. extract_landmarks.py reads the letter folders and writes the landmark table. A missed hand is skipped.
  2. train_mlp.py builds the 95-value rows, splits them, fits the network, and writes normalization, labels, and calibration.
  3. export_tflite.py writes a 32-bit model and an 8-bit model. The phone uses the 32-bit file.
  4. sync_model_bundle.py copies one consistent set into the Android assets. A new model with an old normalization file would be a broken release, so the files move together.
  5. verify_pipeline_parity.py checks that the Kotlin features and the Python features agree.

A separate script can train a short recurrent network on sequences from the same table. That model is a comparison prototype. The installed application uses the single-frame network plus the Z path. Shipping the recurrent model would not make the system translate sentences.

Git records the source, the model bundle, and the tests. It does not record the photograph archive, the virtual environment, the local SDK path, or the release package itself. GitHub holds the source and the installable package. There is no application server and no hosted database.

3.8 Implementation Plan

In one breath. Implementation is split into modules that can be built and checked on their own: data preparation, training and export, the application shell, inference, spelling, and release. Deployment is a file copy. There is no server to roll out.

Table 3.21, the five stages

  1. Skeleton. Camera, landmarks, classifier, both screens, Save and Clear. Exit: a letter can be shown from a live hand.
  2. Training set. Replace the model with one trained on SignAlphaSet. Exit: the phone assets and the training files share input size 95.
  3. Camera switch. Double-tap target and mirrored front-camera frames. Exit: front and back can both be opened.
  4. Letter policy. Gates, the O-versus-C gap, the Z path, three-frame display, six-frame record. Exit: the unit tests for those rules pass. This stage was repeated, because the first easier rule showed letters when nobody had signed.
  5. Release. Debug package published for install, source pushed for teammates. Exit: the package installs over the previous application, because the package name stays com.sign2text.

The distributed package is a debug build, so shrinking and obfuscation are off. That is enough for a class install. It is not a Play Store release, and the chapter does not describe it as one.

Backup, Table 3.22

  • Source, tests, and the model bundle: Git on the main branch. A pull restores the app and the shipped model together.
  • Installable package: the GitHub release asset. Install does not require a build machine.
  • SignAlphaSet photographs and the landmark CSV: kept on the training computer, not in Git. The scripts can rebuild the CSV from the photographs.
  • Spelled words and camera frames: no backup, because they are not stored.

Security, said as a list

The aim is to keep the signer’s video on the phone, not to hide the model. Anyone who unpacks the package can read the model file. The controls are: the camera starts only after permission; frames, landmarks, and letters are not uploaded; the spelled word is not written to disk; only the welcome screen is exported; the word list never receives the camera image. A lost or shared phone can still show whatever word is on the screen at that moment. The app does not add a lock screen of its own. Closing the recognition screen clears the spelled word.

3.9 Testing and Evaluation Procedures

In one breath. We use four levels, and a pass at one level does not replace a pass at the next. Unit tests check the rules without a camera. The build check confirms the package and the six files. The offline photo check confirms the exported network on held-out still images. The device script is a person signing at a physical arm64 phone.

Say this before any accuracy number: the still-photo score checks the pipeline. SignAlphaSet contains many near-duplicate frames of the same pose, and a random split can place near-copies on both sides. A very high photo score can appear even when a new hand, a new room, or a moving sign is harder. A perfect or near-perfect photo score is a reason to inspect duplicates. It is not a claim that every signer will be read correctly. Live results belong to the device procedure and are reported apart from the photo score.

A blank on a poorly formed sign is a pass of the rejection rule. A blank on a clear, held, palm-forward sign is a miss. Blanks on idle hands in D-01 and D-02 are successes and are reported separately from letter accuracy. Front-camera repeats are reported beside the back-camera result. The two are not averaged until both have been shown. No significance test is planned for a volunteer sample of this size. The result describes what that build did.

Participants are volunteer signers. The developers are included, and classmates may take part if they agree. The chapter does not claim a fixed sample size or an ethics-board approval number, because the evaluation is of a software build rather than a clinical study. Each person is told that the camera stays on the phone, that no video is saved, and that they may stop at any time. The score sheet stores the letter outcome only: correct, substitution, or blank. It does not store a photograph. Clear is used before the phone is handed to the next person.

Unit tests you can name

  • Letter acceptance: a weak C, about 0.24, stays blank. A close second letter does not replace a clearer first letter. A drawn Z wins. A closed hand the network calls C, with gap 0.18, is shown as O. An open hand the network calls O, with gap 0.67, is shown as C.
  • Z path: a drawn Z is accepted, including the mirrored direction. A still finger, a straight line, and a curled hand are rejected.
  • Spelling buffer: five frames of the same letter do not enter the word. The sixth frame enters it once. Save, a new word, and Clear behave as specified.
  • Word list: a listed word is found, a missing word is not, and prefix checks match the trie.

Table 3.26, threats — say the treatment, not only the problem

  • Near-duplicate images inflate still-photo accuracy. Treatment: the photo score is a pipeline check, and the device script is required.
  • Few signers in the public set. A new hand can fall outside the training poses. Treatment: reported as a limit. No signer-level split was available.
  • Closed fists missed by the detector, especially O and some of M, N, S, and T. Treatment: the phone threshold is 0.20. A remaining miss of that kind is a detection miss, not a letter-rule miss.
  • Using the emulator as if it were a phone. x86_64 cannot load the hand tracker. Recognition results from that emulator are excluded. An open preview is not a recognition test.
  • Thresholds tuned on the same people who demo the app. The gates can fit one room and one hand. Treatment: the thresholds are written in Table 3.16 and covered by tests, so a later change is visible. A broader signer study is future work. It is not a hidden result of this chapter.

When a build is ready to hand out

The unit tests pass. The debug package builds and contains the matching six files. Calibration temperature is 1.0 and the input size is 95 in both the model and the normalization file. D-01, D-02, D-06, D-07, and D-08 have been tried on a physical arm64 phone. D-03 through D-05 are the accuracy script and are reported with their misses. The photo hold-out may sit beside those device results. It may not replace them.
