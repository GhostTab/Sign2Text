Technical Background and Methodology

Sign2Text: Real-Time American Sign Language Alphabet Recognition on Android

Christian Sadial Eba · Zeben Nueva Ecija Realizan · Raquel Villaflor Bayle
Erica Cano Policarpio · Rodgina Trogon Ybañez

This chapter describes how Sign2Text was specified, designed, built, and prepared for evaluation. The application reads one hand from a phone camera and shows the matching letter of the American Sign Language (ASL) fingerspelling alphabet, from A to Z. The model is trained on a computer. After installation, recognition runs on the phone, and no image, landmark, or letter is sent to a server.

The description follows the software that was built. The researchers repeated requirements, design, construction, test, release, and feedback. The better-lit training set, the camera switch, the stricter rule for a resting hand, and the geometric check that separates O from C each came from a later round of that work.

3.1 Project / Research Design

3.1.1 Type of study

The study is applied research and development. The practical question is whether a phone can turn a held ASL letter into text without a network, and whether the screen can stay blank when a hand is only resting in view of the camera. The result is a working Android application and the training procedure that produced its classifier. A still-photo score alone does not answer that question. Live signing, and the rules that accept or reject a frame, are part of the design.

The approach fits the problem because fingerspelling of a letter is a pose that can be held, so a single-frame classifier and a phone camera are enough for the task the researchers set. Keeping the model on the device meets the privacy requirement and the offline requirement together. A server would have required an account, added network delay, and kept a copy of the hand. A waterfall plan would also have been a poor fit. The blank screen for a resting hand, and the split between O and C, were found only after a build had been tried.

The work is divided into two phases, shown in Table 3.1.

Table 3.1. The two phases of the study
PhaseWhere it runsWhat it produces
Offline developmentComputer, PythonLandmark table, trained network, normalization values, calibration file, TensorFlow Lite model
Online useAndroid phoneA letter on screen, and, if spelling is on, a word built from letters the signer holds

The phone does not retrain the network. It loads one fixed bundle copied into the application at build time. Python and Kotlin use the same landmark mathematics, so a feature computed during training is the same feature the phone computes from a live hand.

3.1.2 Objectives that the design had to meet

  1. Show one ASL letter, A–Z, from a single hand in a live camera frame.
  2. Keep recognition on the device after the application is installed.
  3. Leave the display blank when the hand is present but the letter is not clear.
  4. Record a letter into a spelled word only after the signer holds it.
  5. Treat a closed circle as O and an open curve as C.
  6. Accept Z both as a still pose and as a Z drawn with the index finger.
  7. Let the signer use the front or the back camera.

3.1.3 Scope

The system covers one-handed ASL fingerspelling of the 26 English letters. The signer faces the palm toward the lens and holds the pose. When spelling mode is on, the application appends those letters into a string and can check the string against a local English word list. The interface is portrait Android, API level 26 and above.

ASL grammar, facial expression, and whole-word signs are outside the scope. A waved greeting for “Hi” is a different sign from the fingerspelled letters H and I. The source collection also contains about 300 short videos, including Hello, Thank You, Sorry, Yes, and No (Garg et al., 2025). The researchers did not use those videos. A classifier trained on still alphabet photographs cannot recognize a moving word sign, and this study does not claim that result.

In the still images, J is a pinky pose held in place, and many Z frames show a pointing index rather than a stroke drawn in the air. The phone therefore adds a separate path check for a drawn Z. A flicking J is outside the present model.

3.1.4 Training data

An earlier alphabet collection was set aside because the photographs were dark and the hand was not shown the way a person signs toward a phone. The released model uses SignAlphaSet, version 3 (Garg et al., 2025), from the Kaggle redistribution prepared by Meo under the same Creative Commons Attribution 4.0 license. The copy used here has 26 folders, A through Z, with 1,000 JPEG images in each folder. Each image is 296 × 296 pixels. Palms face the camera, and both left and right hands appear. The published description reports variation in hand shape, lighting, and signer.

Every image was passed through the same MediaPipe Hand Landmarker used on the phone, and a row was kept only when a hand was found. Of the 26,000 images, 25,682 produced a landmark row and 318 did not. O was the letter most often missed at extraction: 709 of its 1,000 images yielded a hand, because a closed fist is easier for the detector to miss. Class weights during training compensate for that imbalance. Extraction used a minimum detection confidence of 0.35. On the phone the same detector is set to 0.20 so that a closed O, M, N, S, or T can still be found in a live frame.

3.1.6 Representation of the hand

The classifier does not receive pixels. MediaPipe Hands returns 21 landmarks, each with an x, y, and z (Zhang et al., 2020). Those 63 numbers are translated so the wrist is the origin, scaled by the longest wrist-to-landmark distance in the image plane, and mirrored when the middle-finger knuckle falls on the left of the wrist. That last step places a left hand in the same frame as a right hand.

Twenty distances from the wrist to the other landmarks, and twelve offsets from the thumb tip to the proximal interphalangeal joints of the four fingers, are then added. The model input is 63 + 20 + 12 = 95 numbers. The extra 32 numbers describe fist letters, where the raw coordinates of a closed hand look alike and the useful difference is where the thumb sits under the fingers. Before the network, each of the 95 values is converted to a z-score using the mean and standard deviation of the training split.

3.1.7 Ethics and data handling

The camera is used only after the person allows it. Frames are processed in memory. They are not stored, and they are not uploaded. The training photographs remain under CC BY 4.0 and are credited to Garg, Kasar, Kashyap, Vats, Sharma, and Hange. The application has no account, no analytics server, and no remote database.

3.2 Software Development Methodology

3.2.1 Method adopted

The researchers followed iterative and incremental development (Sommerville, 2016). A small system was released, used, and then changed. Requirements were not frozen before the first build, and the first model was not treated as the last one. A waterfall sequence would have hidden failures that appeared only when a real hand was held in front of the camera: a resting hand becoming a letter, O being read as C, and taps on the preview failing to switch the camera.

Each cycle has six steps, and feedback from one cycle becomes the input to the next. Figure 3.1 shows the form of that cycle. Table 3.4 lists the cycles that changed the product.

Table 3.4. Development cycles and what each one changed
CycleWhat use showedWhat was built next

3. CameraThe preview surface swallowed taps, so a button on the picture did not flip the camera.A full-screen tap target. A double tap switches front and back. The front-camera frame is mirrored before analysis.
4. Hard lettersC, M, N, O, T, and Z were rejected when the sign was slightly off. A later build then printed letters when nobody had signed.Those six letters may appear at a slightly lower score, but only when they are clearly first. A second-place letter can no longer replace the first.
5. O read as CA closed circle and an open curve share a silhouette.If the network already prefers C or O, the thumb-to-index gap chooses between them.

3.2.2 How a cycle was controlled

Source changes sit on the main branch of the project repository. Behavior that can be checked without a camera, namely letter acceptance, the Z path, and the spelling buffer, is locked by unit tests before the interface is trusted. A debug Android package is the release file. Classmates who only need the application install that package. Teammates who need the source pull it and open the Android project under mobile/android, rather than at the root of the repository. The machine-specific Android SDK path is kept out of version control.

The work is incremental because each cycle left a usable application in place. The spelling screen, the camera, and the model were not postponed until a final stage. It is iterative because thresholds were revised after they failed in use, instead of being declared correct at the start.

3.3 Requirements Analysis

3.3.1 Users and roles

The application is used by two roles. They may be the same person.

  • Signer. Fingerspells one letter at a time, palm toward the lens, and may turn spelling on, save a word, delete a letter, or clear the line. The signer also chooses the front or back camera.
  • Reader. Reads the letter or the saved word on the screen. The reader does not need an account and does not operate a second device.

The phone may be held by the signer, using the front camera, or aimed at the signer, using the back camera. After a one-time install, the application is expected to work indoors in ordinary light and without an internet connection. The people who build the software are not a user role while the application is running. They use the repository and Android Studio, which are development tools in Section 3.7.

3.4.1 Architectural style

3.4.3 Why the hand is reduced to landmarks

A network trained on raw pixels would have to learn skin color, background, and camera distance along with the letter. Landmarks already remove most of that variation. The phone and the training script share one definition of the 95 values, and the parity script in the repository checks that the two definitions agree. The resulting network has four dense layers and runs with TensorFlow Lite, so the application does not depend on a graphics server or a connection.

The same choice leaves some letters difficult. M and N place the fingers in nearly the same spots, so they remain easy to confuse. A motion that is not in the still photographs, such as a J flick, is invisible to the network. The researchers accepted those limits. For Z, a second detector covers part of the motion.

The installed application contains the interface, the Kotlin pipeline, and the files in Table 3.10. Nothing else is fetched at launch. CameraX, MediaPipe, and TensorFlow Lite are compiled into the package, and they are the integrations. At runtime the application does not call a website, a login service, or a cloud model.

3.5.4 Feature design

Table 3.13. Contents of the 95-value model input
BlockCountDefinition
Landmarks6321 points × (x, y, z), wrist at the origin, scaled, mirrored into a right-hand frame
Wrist distances20Distance from the wrist to landmarks 1 through 20
Thumb relations12Index, middle, ring, and pinky middle joints, each minus the thumb tip, as x, y, and z

The gap used for O and C is not one of the 95 trained features. It is the distance between landmark 4 (thumb tip) and landmark 8 (index tip) in the wrist-scaled frame, computed before the z-score step. On the extracted SignAlphaSet rows the two letters separate cleanly on this distance. For C, the 10th percentile of the gap is about 0.52 and the median is about 0.67. For O, the 90th percentile is about 0.20 and the median is about 0.18. The rules use 0.42 and 0.48, which fall in the empty interval between those distributions. A gap between 0.42 and 0.48 does not force either letter.

Temperature scaling was fitted on the validation logits. The fit reached the bottom of the search grid, 0.2, because near-duplicate frames make the network look more certain than a new signer will. A temperature of 0.2 would have pushed almost every frame through the confidence gate. 3.5.6 Decision design

Figure 3.5 shows the order of the rules. A drawn Z is decided first. If the thumb-to-index gap then returns O or C, the later score rules are not consulted. A gap between 0.42 and 0.48 does not reject the frame. The lenient rule can still accept the model’s top letter. Any letter that is accepted, including Z and the O-versus-C result, is shown only after 3 agreeing frames. It is written into the word only when spelling mode is on and that same letter has stayed on screen for 6 frames.

Table 3.16. Numeric rules in the released application

The lead is the probability of the first letter minus the probability of the second. Entropy is the Shannon entropy of the 26 smoothed probabilities. When entropy is high, the network is spread across many letters, and the frame is rejected even if the top score looks moderate.

Spelling mode does not save a word by itself. An open palm was previously treated as a confirm gesture. Many real letters also look open, and that gesture was clearing a word before the signer had finished. Save is now an explicit control. Taking the saved string clears the buffer but remembers the last displayed letter, so the same hold is not written again immediately.

3.5.7 Z path in more detail

The detector stores the index fingertip while the index is extended and the other fingers are curled. The scale is the distance from the wrist to the middle-finger knuckle. The path is resampled to 15 points and split into three segments. The first and third must be mostly horizontal and must travel the same way. The middle segment must move downward and the opposite way in x. A still pointing finger fails the length test, and a straight line fails the direction test. Because the front-camera frame is mirrored before this check, a Z drawn on either camera is tested in the same orientation.

3.6 Database Design

3.6.1 Design decision

Sign2Text does not use a database server, and it does not use SQLite. That choice follows from the requirements in Section 3.3. Recognition has to work offline, spelled text is needed only for the current visit, and camera frames must not be kept. A schema of users, sessions, and stored images would have created data the application is not meant to keep.

The system keeps a small set of files, each with a fixed job, and a few values that live only in memory. Figure 3.7 shows that arrangement. Table 3.17 is the data dictionary.

3.6.2 Entities and relationships

Figure 3.8 is the logical schema. Because there is no SQL database to draw, the entities are the files and the in-memory values the application actually keeps. Cardinality is written on each line. A training image produces at most one landmark row, since a missed hand is skipped. Many landmark rows train one model bundle, and one bundle is loaded into one running session. 3.6.3 The landmark table as a relation

During training, the landmark file is a single relation in first normal form. Each cell holds one value, and the letter does not depend on a second table. The file that was used has no separate signer table, so a signer-level split was not available and the split is stratified by letter. The script can use a group column when the folders contain one folder per person. That option was not the split used for the shipped model. The limitation is stated in Section 3.9.

3.6.4 The word list

The word list is not a relational table, and the user does not update it. At startup each line becomes a path in a trie, and a node marks the end of a word. containsWord is true only at a terminal node. hasPrefix is true when the path exists, which lets the interface say that the letters so far could still grow into a word. If the file cannot be read, spelling still works and the hint is omitted.

3.6.5 What is deliberately not stored

  • Camera frames and landmark traces, except the few samples inside the 1.8-second Z window, which are dropped after a decision or a reset.
  • A history of recognized letters across visits.
  • Accounts, device identifiers for a backend, or crash reports sent off the phone.
  • The raw SignAlphaSet photographs inside the Android package. Only the trained bundle is installed.

3.7.2 Training toolchain

The scripts are part of the method, because another person can rerun them. The order is fixed.

  1. extract_landmarks.py reads the letter folders and writes the landmark table. A hand that is not found is skipped, not filled with zeros.
  2. train_mlp.py builds the 95-value rows, splits them, fits the network, and writes the normalization file, the label map, and a calibration file.
  3. export_tflite.py writes a 32-bit float model and an 8-bit model. The phone uses the 32-bit file.
  4. sync_model_bundle.py copies one consistent set of files into the Android assets. Mixing a new model with an old normalization file is a broken release, so the files move together.
  5. verify_pipeline_parity.py checks that the Kotlin feature definition and the Python feature definition agree.

A separate script can train a short recurrent network on sequences from the same table. That model is a comparison prototype. The installed application uses the single-frame network plus the Z path. Shipping the recurrent model would not make the system translate sentences.

3.7.3 Build and release tooling

Android Studio opens mobile/android. The debug build is produced with Gradle assembleDebug, and the unit tests are run with testDebugUnitTest. Git records the source, the model bundle, and the tests. It does not record the photograph archive, the virtual environment, the local SDK path, or the release package itself. The installable file is a GitHub release asset named Sign2Text-debug.apk, so a person who only wants the application does not need the repository. There is no application server and no hosted database. GitHub holds the source and the installable package. Section 3.8 states how that package is produced and what is backed up.

3.8.3 Deployment

Deployment is a file copy. There is no server to roll out. The debug package is attached to a GitHub release, and a person who only wants the application installs that package. Installing again over the same package name, com.sign2text, replaces the previous build. A teammate who needs the source pulls the main branch and opens mobile/android in Android Studio. The machine-specific SDK path stays in local.properties and is not part of the deployment. The recognition activity is not exported, so another application cannot start it. The welcome activity is the only launcher entry.

The distributed package is a debug build, so shrinking and obfuscation are off. That is enough for a class install. It is not a step that prepares the application for a public store, and this chapter does not describe it as one.

3.8.5 Security

The security aim is to keep the signer’s video on the phone, not to hide the model. Anyone who unpacks the package can read the model file. The controls that match the requirements are listed below.

  • The camera starts only after the operating system permission is granted.
  • Frames, landmarks, and letters are not uploaded. The application opens no account and calls no recognition API.
  • The spelled word is not written to disk.
  • Only the welcome screen is exported. The recognition screen cannot be launched from outside the application.
  • The word list is a local file used for a hint. It does not receive the camera image.

A lost or shared phone can still show whatever word is on the screen at that moment. The application does not add a lock screen of its own. Closing the recognition screen clears the spelled word.

3.9 Testing and Evaluation Procedures

3.9.1 Levels of evidence

Four levels are used. They answer different questions, and a pass at one level does not replace a pass at the next.

Table 3.23. Evaluation levels
LevelQuestionWhere it runs
UnitDo the letter rules, the Z path, and the spelling buffer behave as specified on known inputs?JVM, no camera
BuildDoes the application compile, and are the model files inside the package?Gradle
Offline photosDoes the exported network reproduce letters on held-out still images from the same collection?Python, on the landmark table

3.9.2 Unit tests

The tests record failures from earlier cycles, so the same failure is caught if it returns.

Table 3.24. Unit-test obligations
Test classCases that must hold
LetterAcceptanceTestA weak C, about 0.24, stays blank. A close second letter does not replace a clearer first letter. A drawn Z wins. A closed hand the network calls C, with gap 0.18, is shown as O. An open hand the network calls O, with gap 0.67, is shown as C.
ZStrokeDetectorTestA drawn Z is accepted, including the mirrored direction. A still finger, a straight line, and a curled hand are rejected.
LetterSequenceAccumulatorTestSave then a new word then Clear behaves as specified. With the record length set to 6, five frames of the same letter do not enter the word, and the sixth frame enters it once.
WordDictionaryTestA listed word is found. A missing word is not. Prefix checks match the trie.

A change to a threshold in Section 3.5 is complete only after these tests are updated to the new rule and the Gradle unit-test task passes.

3.9.3 Build checks

Before a package is handed out, assembleDebug must finish, and the package must contain the six runtime files in Table 3.10. The float model must declare 95 inputs. The normalization file and the calibration file must carry the same input size, and the temperature in the shipped calibration file must be 1.0.

3.9.4 Offline model check, and what it is not

The training script holds out 10 percent of the landmark rows, stratified by letter, after a separate 10 percent validation split. The offline check scores the exported TensorFlow Lite model on that hold-out, using the same z-score and the same temperature file the phone uses. A report for a model run should include overall accuracy, accuracy for each letter, and the pairs that are confused. The pairs to watch are M with N, U with R, and C with O, because their landmarks are close.

That number checks the pipeline. It is not the live accuracy of the application. SignAlphaSet contains many near-duplicate frames of the same pose, and a random split can place near-copies on both sides. A very high still-photo score can therefore appear even when a new hand, a new room, or a moving sign is harder. In this study a perfect or near-perfect photo score is a reason to inspect duplicates. It is not a claim that every signer will be read correctly. Live results belong to the device procedure below and are reported apart from the photo score.

The same caution applies to J and Z. A high score on a still pinky, or on a still pointing finger, does not measure a J flick or a Z drawn in the air. The Z path has its own unit tests, and a live drawing of Z is part of the device script.

3.9.5 Participants and instruments

Device testing uses volunteer signers. The developers are included, and classmates may take part if they agree to try the script. This chapter does not claim a fixed sample size or an ethics-board approval number, because the evaluation is of a software build rather than a clinical study. Each person is told that the camera stays on the phone, that no video is saved, and that they may stop at any time.

The instruments are the tests already in the repository and the script in Table 3.25.

  • JUnit 4, through testDebugUnitTest, for the rules in Table 3.24.
  • Gradle assembleDebug, for the package and its asset files.
  • The Python evaluation script on the held-out landmark rows, for the pipeline check.
  • A score sheet for the phone: letter, camera (front or back), and outcome (correct, substitution, or blank). The sheet records the outcome, not a photograph.

3.9.6 Device procedure

Recognition is tested on a physical Android phone, API 26 or newer, with an arm64 processor. A 64-bit Intel emulator is not a valid recognition device for this project, because the MediaPipe native library is not in the x86_64 build and the hand landmarker cannot load. The camera preview may still open on that emulator, but an open preview is not a recognition test. The signer is asked to face the palm toward the lens, in ordinary indoor light, and to follow Table 3.25 in order.

Table 3.25. Device test script
IDActionExpected result
D-01Open the app, allow the camera, put no hand in frame.Preview runs. The letter area stays blank. Nothing is written into a word.
D-02Rest an open hand in frame without forming a letter. Wait several seconds.The display stays blank, or returns to blank. The spelling line does not gain a letter.
D-03Hold each letter A–Z, palm to the lens, for about one second.The matching letter appears. Note any substitution, especially M/N and U/R.
D-04Form O with the fingertips touching, then C with a clear gap.O then C. A slightly imperfect closure may still read O if the gap is under 0.42 and the network already prefers C or O.
D-05Point the index and draw a Z.Z appears and stays briefly. A straight wave does not.
D-06Double-tap the preview.The camera changes and a message names front or back. A second double tap returns.
D-07Turn spelling on. Flash a letter and drop the hand. Then hold a letter.The flash does not join the word. The held letter joins once.
D-08Spell a short word, tap Save, spell another, tap Backspace, tap Clear.Save shows the word and empties the line. Backspace removes one letter. Clear removes the line and the saved word.
D-09Turn the network off and repeat D-03 for one letter.The letter still appears. Recognition does not wait on a connection.

Each letter in D-03 is recorded as correct, substituted, or blank. A blank on a poorly formed sign counts as a pass of the rejection rule. A blank on a clear, held, palm-forward sign counts as a miss. Substitutions are kept in a confusion list. The procedure is repeated on the front camera for at least the letters that failed on the back camera, because mirroring and the different view can change the result.

3.9.7 Analysis

Unit tests and the build check are scored pass or fail, and they are not averaged. The offline photo check reports overall accuracy, accuracy for each letter, and the confused pairs, with M/N, U/R, and C/O listed separately. That report is labeled as a pipeline check.

On the phone, letter accuracy uses only the clear, held, palm-forward attempts in D-03, computed as correct attempts divided by attempts. Substitutions stay in a confusion list so that they remain visible beside the percent. Blanks on idle hands in D-01 and D-02 are reported separately, because a blank there is a success, while blanks on a clear sign count as misses. Front-camera repeats are reported beside the back-camera result, and the two are not averaged until both have been shown. No significance test is planned for a volunteer sample of this size. The result describes what that build did.

3.9.8 When a build is ready to hand out

A build is ready for classmates, and ready to install, when all of the following are true.

  • The unit tests in Table 3.24 pass.
  • The debug package builds and contains the matching model, normalization, calibration, labels, hand landmarker, and word list.
  • Calibration temperature is 1.0 and the input size is 95 in both the model and the normalization file.
  • D-01, D-02, D-06, D-07, and D-08 have been tried on a physical arm64 phone for that build. D-03 through D-05 are the accuracy script and are reported with their misses, not replaced by the photo score.

The photo hold-out may be reported beside those device results, but it may not replace them. Sentence-level signs remain out of scope until a video set is trained with a model built for motion. Retraining the present alphabet network on the SignAlphaSet still images does not create that capability.

3.9.9 Ethics and privacy safeguards

The camera permission dialog is the technical consent for capture. Before a volunteer starts Table 3.25, the person is also told what the trial is for and that the phone may be put down at any time. Frames are processed in memory and are not written to a file, an image log, or a server. The score sheet stores the letter outcome only. The spelled-word trial uses short test strings chosen for the script, and Clear is used before the phone is handed to the next person. Credit for the training photographs stays with the SignAlphaSet authors under CC BY 4.0. Those photographs are not shown from the phone, and they are not part of the volunteer trial.
